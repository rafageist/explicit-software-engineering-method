# 1. Why This Is a Method

This work is intentionally framed as a method because it targets engineering accountability, not workflow coordination or academic justification. The distinction is operational: it determines what can be evaluated and how compliance can be assessed across different organizations and practices. A process can organize work, but a method determines whether the engineering result is accountable.

## 1.1 Methodology vs Method vs Process
Process is an ordered sequence of activities that supports coordination and delivery. Methodology is the study and justification of methods, providing conceptual explanations and rationale. A framework offers a reusable organizational structure that packages roles, practices, and conventions, while a philosophy provides guiding principles that shape judgment without imposing obligations. Each of these can improve organization and understanding, but none of them, by themselves, enforce engineering accountability.

For example, a team can start from code or from models and still follow the same process; the process does not determine whether the reasoning behind those choices remains accountable.

## 1.2 Why "Method" Fits
A method provides normative obligations and consequences for missing or insufficient engineering work. It makes evaluability independent of execution order, so work can be assessed even when teams follow different workflows or operate under different organizational constraints. This makes the method applicable across heterogeneous contexts without prescribing how work must be scheduled.

That distinction matters when requirements are discovered late. A process can accommodate late discovery, but a method determines whether the resulting changes are still accountable.

## 1.3 Dependency Without Rigidity
Engineering has logical dependencies: a problem must be understood before a solution can be validated. Acknowledging those dependencies does not require a fixed process sequence. The method constrains outcomes and relationships, not activity order, and allows teams to discover and revise understanding without violating accountability.

This is visible when decisions are made under pressure. The method does not forbid them; it requires that their consequences remain traceable and accountable even when timing is constrained.

## 1.4 Separation of Concerns
This approach is not a rejection of Agile, Scrum, RUP, or any other organizational approach. It separates concerns: processes organize work; a method defines the obligations that make engineering outcomes accountable. The method can coexist with any organizational approach because it does not prescribe how teams should coordinate.

The separation is practical: organizational choices optimize coordination, while engineering accountability requires a stable basis for evaluation regardless of coordination style.

## 1.5 Method as Evaluative Function
At its core, a method can act as an evaluative function: given engineering artifacts and context, it can determine whether obligations were met and where accountability is missing. This perspective motivates a method over process and prepares the ground for distinguishing software that merely works from software that qualifies as an engineered product.

Taken together, these points show why a method is the correct abstraction: it preserves accountability across different ways of organizing work and makes engineering outcomes evaluable. With that foundation, the next step is to clarify the boundary between software that functions and software that qualifies as an engineered product.
